\subsection*{[1] The frontier, and a model on it}

Removing the study a cell came from and keeping the phase of its donor's
cycle are, in this corpus, the same operation pulling in opposite
directions. Rather than fix a compromise, we varied the two terms that
control it and measured where each setting lands: the strength of the
adversarial pressure on the latent, and the price the latent pays for what
it stores. Six models, three adversarial strengths at two latent prices,
registered before any of them was trained.

The two knobs do different things (Fig.~1b, c). Raising the adversarial
strength from the weakest setting moves the whole trade-off: donor recovery
falls from 0.5837 to 0.5188 while cross-donor phase is unchanged at 0.7346
against 0.7337. Raising it further buys nothing --- at the strongest setting
donor recovery is 0.5277, no better, and phase has fallen to 0.7146. The
adversary therefore pays until it stops paying, and the point where it stops
is visible rather than assumed. The latent price behaves differently. Making
the latent expensive drives donor recovery down to 0.3975 and study mixing to
0.5129, the lowest of any model we trained, and takes cross-donor phase down
with it to 0.6033. It does not move the trade-off; it slides a model along
it.

Everything reported below comes from one of these six, the model at the
middle adversarial strength and the cheap latent. It recovers phase across
donors at 0.7346 against a chance rate of 0.370, recovers cell family at
0.9820, and leaves donor identity at 0.5188 and study mixing at 0.6043. It
is not the most donor-blind model on the frontier and it is not the most
biologically faithful; it is the point at which neither has been sacrificed
to the other, and it is named here so that every number in the rest of this
paper has a model behind it.

\subsection*{[2] \elaj\ against the field}

A sixteen-dimensional representation that forgets donors is easy to build
and worthless; one that keeps the cycle while doing so is the claim. We
compared against eleven methods on held-out donors: linear decomposition,
two variational models, two batch-correction tools, and four pretrained
single-cell foundation models (Fig.~3).

No other method tested holds as much of the cycle. Cross-donor phase
agreement reaches 0.7346 against 0.7053 for scVI, 0.6971 for scANVI and
0.6906 for a sixteen-component linear decomposition, with chance at 0.370.
At the same time no other method discards as much of where a cell came
from: study mixing is 0.4712 against 0.6284 for the nearest competitor, and
donor recovery is lower than every method we tested. The two are usually
traded against one another, and the comparison was designed so that they
could be seen separately.

Width is not what produces this. The four foundation models carry between
sixteen and a hundred and ninety-two times our dimensions and sit near chance
on phase --- 0.4869 at five hundred and twelve dimensions, 0.4372 at two
hundred and fifty-six --- while a sixteen-component linear decomposition of
the same data reaches 0.6906. Cell family is the one axis on which we are
matched rather than ahead, at 0.9841 against 0.9896 and 0.9835 for the two
methods that were trained on cell-type labels; supervision is declared per
method in the figure, and ours is the menstrual phase.

Disease is where every method including ours is weak. Our best cross-donor
figure is 0.3787 and three of the baselines reach higher, none of them
convincingly. That a representation can carry the cycle and cell identity
this well while carrying disease this poorly is a result about the disease
rather than about any one model, and we return to it below.
